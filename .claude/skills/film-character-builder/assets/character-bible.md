# Story character bible

Develop who the person is and what their choices do to the story before specifying their performance. Pair this with the wardrobe bible, performance sheet and voice sheet as needed.

Contradictions need a cause. “Generous with practical help, guarded about accepting it” is playable when the scene tests both sides. “Mysterious, damaged, charismatic” alone does not tell a writer what the person does. A wound, secret, trauma or redemption arc is optional, not a requirement for depth.

## Relationships and agency

| Other character | Shared history that matters now | What each wants from the other | Imbalance / friction | How the relationship changes through an action |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| | | | | |

- What would this character do if the protagonist never arrived?
- How does their action change the next beat instead of merely reacting to the plot?
- What does the other person misunderstand about them?
- What do they understand accurately, despite the conflict?

Give supporting and opposing characters their own credible aims. Do not reduce a partner to a lesson, a villain to a label, or a parent to a source of exposition.

## Behaviour and screen presence

- First action that reveals something before explanation:
- Relevant competence in an ordinary task:
- One or two personal habits, used when motivated:
- How they seek attention, avoid it or manage distance:
- Speech tendencies: sentence shape, directness, avoidance and rhythm; detailed voice identity belongs in the voice sheet:
- A physical or verbal response that changes under pressure:
- Useful ordinary detail unrelated to the central conflict:
- What should not be exaggerated into a repeated mannerism:

Appearance can support identity without functioning as a moral code. Do not infer personality, accent, nationality or history from a reference person's face or clothes. Distinctiveness need not mean a scar, unusual eye colour, a tragic secret or conspicuous styling.

For a short film, make the essential change readable in a few actions. Keep unused biography out of the script. For a story spanning years, track age, relationships, abilities, habits and voice deliberately; avoid treating ageing as a different identity. Some characters remain steadfast and change the situation instead of changing themselves.

## Handoffs

- **Script:** necessary choices, relationship pressure and observable story turn; mark any proposed change to approved plot.
- **Performance:** tactics, triggers, listening and recovery; avoid reducing the person to one emotion.
- **Voice:** confirmed language/voice choices and open casting questions; no accent inferred from appearance.
- **Styling:** self-presentation, practical needs, possessions, resources and any intended changes of situation. Describe what they choose to project, not only what they can afford.
- **Identity references:** canonical portrait and any proposed derivatives; preserve the selected face when changing clothes.

## Character check

- Does their choice cause an important beat?
- Is the contradiction visible rather than explained only in biography?
- Are competence, vulnerability and desire specific to this person?
- Can the audience understand the essential relationship within the runtime?
- Are both sides of the relationship more than their labels?
- Does the ending test something established at the beginning?
- Could the same description fit every character? If so, replace generic traits with different choices and tactics.
